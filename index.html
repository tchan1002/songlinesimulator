What is an Aboriginal Songline?

Australian Museum: "Songlines are the Aboriginal walking routes that crossed the country. They were created by ancestral spirits, and marked by sacred sites. Singing the 'songs' in the correct order enabled people to navigate vast distances."1

Bruce Chatwin, The Songlines: "Each totemic ancestor, while travelling through the country, was thought to have scattered a trail of words and musical notes along the line of his footprints... These Dreaming-tracks lay over the land as 'ways' of communication between the most far-flung tribes."2

National Museum of Australia: "A songline is a map of Country that is conveyed through songs, stories, dance and painting. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, a songline is often connected to a Dreaming story and can traverse thousands of kilometres."3

Sources:
1. Australian Museum - Songlines
2. Bruce Chatwin, The Songlines (1987)
3. National Museum of Australia - Songlines and Country

This project reimagines transit routes as songlines—not claiming indigenous knowledge, but exploring how sound and direction can create richer connections to places.

How the Encoding Works

Each transit type gets its own sound:

  • 🚌 Bus: Deep bass tone. Pitch = route number (e.g., #22 vs #77). Length = number of stops.
  • 🚇 Train: Rhythmic beat. Each CTA line (Red, Blue, etc.) has a set frequency.
  • 🚶 Walking: Fast percussive clicks, like footsteps.

Direction is encoded in pitch and stereo panning:

  • North/South: North = higher pitch, South = lower pitch
  • East/West: East = right ear, West = left ear

Example: If you're going east on the Blue Line, you hear a beat in your right ear. Turn north on a bus? The tone gets higher.

The visual pattern: Overlapping circles (each segment is a "site") with concentric rings showing stops. Connected by curved lines and dots, like traditional Aboriginal art. Color = transit mode, size = duration.

Use headphones to hear the directional audio as you explore your route.
